In July and August 2025 I spent four weeks at Shenzhen Tagen Group as a smart-construction assistant,
through a Taiwan-youth internship program. The plan was two weeks on site and two weeks of desk research.
What I saw in the first two weeks changed what I wanted to read about in the last two.

## AI site safety

The company runs an AI safety platform that fuses daily labour registration, drone patrol imagery and fixed
cameras. Vision models flag missing helmets, unharnessed work at height and unauthorised entry into danger
zones, and match each event to a worker identity, crew roster and gate record.

It records violations well. It does nothing about them in the moment. The open question I kept coming back to:
could wearables and an autonomous platform close that loop, with a drone dispatched for visual confirmation and a
voice warning at the point of violation?

## BIM and LiDAR for prefabricated metro stations

At the BIM centre I sat in on a research discussion for Shenzhen Metro Line 19: using BIM models together with
LiDAR point clouds to raise assembly precision for prefabricated station components. Two pieces stood out.

- **Geometric quality inspection.** Scan the component, register it against the BIM geometry, extract features,
  measure deviation, emit a standard report.
- **Virtual assembly.** Match key-point coordinates between components with Procrustes analysis to compute the
  best fitting pose and docking order before anything is lifted.

The step nobody was taking yet: letting a robot read the same BIM model and point cloud, recognise the component
itself, and adjust its own assembly pose. The data pipeline exists. The autonomy on top of it does not.

## Four sites, four kinds of robot

Across four sites (Hejun Mansion, Baolong biotech base, Tangjia smart manufacturing park, Metro Line 19) I saw
inspection drones, surveying robots, interior plastering robots and floor-levelling robots in actual use.

The floor leveller is the honest example. It needs at least four workers: one to mount sensors and set the
datum, one to mark the levelling boundary, one to guide its path by hand during the pass, and one to patch
whatever it misses. It reduces physical strain. It does not reduce time.

## Three barriers

None of this looked like a motion-control problem. The machines move fine. What they lack is on the other side:

1. **No semantic model of the building.** The robot cannot read the BIM model or the relations between
   components, so a person has to specify the work region and path for every job, and it cannot move itself
   from one component to the next.
2. **No dynamic perception of the site.** Most systems have no vision or depth sensing integrated. They rely on
   pre-placed markers and manual calibration, and fail as soon as material is moved or a view is blocked.
3. **No task-level feedback.** Execution is a fixed script. A plastering robot that meets an uneven wall cannot
   adapt and cannot report how complete the job is; a person fixes it afterwards.

I asked my mentors to turn the remaining two weeks into desk research on exactly these three gaps, and wrote a
technical review that combined the site observations with the current literature. It is, in hindsight, the
reason I now work on task planning that notices when its assumptions no longer match the world.

## Side work: Rhino and Revit

In the evenings I took two BIM-adjacent courses. For *Design Computing: 3D Modeling in Rhinoceros with Python*
the final assignment was a Möbius tower, a helical stack of Möbius-strip modules whose radius, width, twist and
rise are each driven by loops and dictionaries over a 3D surface matrix. For *BIM Fundamentals for Engineers* I
built grids and levels, stairs and ramps, and extracted section views and schedule data in Revit.
